Part one

Recognition from
local features

How much of an image does a decision actually rest on?

Recognition already runs on local evidence

  • Last lecture: BagNet classifies ImageNet from small patches scored independently, with no spatial arrangement at all
  • And scrambling an image into texture patches cost VGG-16 only 90% → 79% — the global layout was carrying little of the decision
  • So: a lot of recognition is available from local features. The question is what, if anything, to do with where they are
Brendel & Bethge (2019)

Three ways to use local features

  • No spatial information — detect local features, ignore where they are
    • Bag of words / bag of features
  • Weak spatial relations — roughly where, not exactly
    • Spatial pyramid models
  • Explicit spatial relations — model the geometry between features
    • Deformable-parts models
    • Keypoint tracking and matching
  • This lecture builds the machinery the third one needs: find the points, then describe them
Part two

Feature detection

Which points are worth describing at all?

Everywhere, or only where it matters

  • Dense — compute a local feature at every position
    • Nothing is missed, and nothing has to be decided in advance
    • Most of it describes blank wall and clear sky
  • Sparse — compute local features only at a few important points
    • Far fewer descriptors to store, compare and match
    • Now "important" has to be defined, and defined the same way in every image

What makes a good feature point?

  • Feature detection — finding important points in an image, also called interest points or keypoints
  • Important means one thing above all: the same point is found again after the image changes — moved, rotated, relit, seen from elsewhere
  • Which makes uniqueness the requirement. Move the patch a few pixels and its contents should already look different — a patch that matches its own neighbours cannot tell you which of them it was

Write "change under a shift" down

__EQ_ENERGYDARK__ __EQ_ENERGYLIGHT__
  • Compare the window against itself, shifted by [u, v], and sum the squared difference
  • w(x, y) is a window function — square, or Gaussian to weight the centre more heavily
  • E(u, v) is small where the shifted window still matches, and large where it does not. E(0, 0) is always zero
  • A good keypoint is a position where E rises steeply for every [u, v]
  • So the shape of E near the origin is the whole signal — and a shape is exactly what a 2×2 matrix can carry

Linearise, and the sum becomes a matrix

  • For small shifts, approximate the shifted intensity with a first-order Taylor expansion:

  • The I(x, y) terms cancel, leaving a quadratic form in u and v:

Everything about the patch is in M

__EQ_STRUCTUREDARK__ __EQ_STRUCTURELIGHT__
  • Built from first derivatives only — no second derivatives, no corner template, nothing to match against
  • Each pixel in the window contributes the outer product of its gradient with itself, weighted by w
  • So M is a summary of which directions the intensity changes in, across the window — and that is the shape of E

On a unit shift, E is an eigenvalue of M

__EQ_QUADDARK__ __EQ_QUADLIGHT__
  • Constrain the shift to unit length — ask which direction to move in, not how far
  • M is a sum of outer products weighted by w ≥ 0, so it is real and symmetric, and positive semi-definite — E is a weighted sum of squares and cannot be negative
  • Symmetric gives two real eigenvalues with orthogonal eigenvectors; semi-definite makes both non-negative
  • Along an eigenvector M only rescales, so E is exactly that eigenvalue:
__EQ_EIGVECDARK__ __EQ_EIGVECLIGHT__
  • So λ2 is a floor under E — to first order, no unit shift in any direction costs less than the smaller eigenvalue:

Both eigenvalues large means a corner

Uniform

Corner

A corner needs both eigenvalues large — so a large product, and a large sum too. Telling it from an edge means penalising the case where the sum is large but the product is not.
Figure: K. Grauman

Harris scores a corner without finding the eigenvalues

__EQ_HARRISDARK__ __EQ_HARRISLIGHT__
  • The determinant and trace are the product and the sum of the eigenvalues — and both come straight from the entries of M, with no eigendecomposition
    • det = np.linalg.det(M)  ·  trace = M.trace()
  • Read the sign: flat gives R ≈ 0; an edge has a big trace but a small determinant, so R goes negative; a corner makes R large and positive
  • k sets how harshly the trace is penalised — determined empirically, around 0.04 – 0.06
Detector: Harris & Stephens (1988)

Harris corner invariances

  • Invariant to translation — the response is computed from gradients in a local window, so it moves with the image
  • Invariant to rotation in the image plane — the eigenvalues of M do not change when the window is rotated, only the eigenvectors do
  • Tolerant to changes in lighting — gradients survive a shift in brightness, and largely survive a change in gain
  • Not invariant to scale — zoom in far enough and a corner becomes a smooth curve, and the window no longer sees a corner at all
  • Scale is what SIFT's detector goes on to solve, by looking for the scale of maximum response

Other ways to score the same matrix

__EQ_ALTDARK__ __EQ_ALTLIGHT__
  • Both avoid Harris's free parameter k. Shi–Tomasi's min(λ1, λ2) says plainly that the weakest direction must still be strong; Brown, Szeliski & Winder's det/tr is the harmonic mean of the two eigenvalues, up to a factor of two
  • And corners are not the only option
    • Blob detectors — respond to a region rather than a point, and come with a natural scale
    • Learned detectors — train a network to propose repeatable points, as SuperPoint does

Feature detection

  • Rather than describing every position, a detector finds a few interest points worth describing
  • The commonest kind is a corner: a point where the window cannot slide in any direction without changing
  • Corners fall out of local gradients alone — build M, score it, threshold
Part three

Feature descriptors

Now say what is there, in a way that survives the next photograph.

A corner is not yet a correspondence

Photographs: Marsilar (CC BY-SA 4.0) and Orgio89 (CC0), via Wikimedia Commons · corners: Harris, k = 0.05

Use local image information to make correspondences

  • The image patch itself — simple, and useless as soon as the lighting or the viewpoint moves
  • Handcrafted descriptors — built to discard exactly the things that should not matter
    • SIFT (Lowe 2004) · GLOH (Mikolajczyk & Schmid 2005)
    • BRIEF (Calonder et al. 2010) · BRISK (Leutenegger et al. 2011) · ORB (Rublee et al. 2011)
  • Learned descriptors — trained so that matching points land near each other
  • They differ in encoding, size and speed, but they are all after the same thing: the same point gives the same code
DeTone, Malisiewicz & Rabinovich (2018)

SIFT: a grid of gradient histograms

  • Take the gradient at every pixel around the interest point, and histogram the orientations in a grid of cells
  • Weight each gradient by its magnitude and by its distance from the centre, then normalise the histograms
Image: D. Lowe

SIFT in numbers

  • Patch 16 × 16 pixels, divided into a 4 × 4 grid of cells
  • 8 orientation bins per cell, Gaussian-weighted from the centre of the patch
  • Two-step normalisation: normalise to sum to 1, truncate at 0.2, normalise again — so no single huge gradient can dominate
  • Descriptor length 4 × 4 × 8 = 128
Lowe (2004)

Where SIFT's invariance comes from

  • Interest points are detected at multiple scales, and the descriptor is built at the scale of maximum response — so the patch covers the same part of the object however far away it is
  • The histogram is then rotated so it is measured relative to the dominant orientation in the patch — which buys rotation invariance for free
Image: D. Lowe

Feature descriptors

  • A descriptor is a code for the patch around an interest point
  • Many handcrafted ones, differing in encoding, speed, size, and which detector they assume
  • The goal in every case is invariance — so the same physical point matches across images taken under different conditions

What you do with "where" is the design choice

  • Nearly all recognition runs on local features; the approaches differ in how much spatial relation between them they keep
  • Bag-of-features — none at all. Enough for category-level recognition, where the parts matter more than the layout
  • Detection and matching — precise positions. Needed when structures have to be matched across images
  • Using geometry to constrain correspondences across images is the next lecture
