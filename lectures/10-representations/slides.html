Part one

Contrastive learning

Making a training signal out of the data itself.

Self-supervised learning

  • Supervised — train from ground-truth labels
  • Unsupervised — train without labels, e.g. clustering
  • Self-supervised — generate the "labels" from the data itself
    • Mask part of an image and train a model to predict the missing part
    • Or: show the model two views of the same photograph and ask which pairs match
  • The advantage is the same in every case: no manual annotation

Representation learning as a classification problem

Where the vocabulary comes from

  • With labels, the columns are a vocabulary: the weight vectors of the classification layer, one per class
    • The target is one-hot — the similarity to the true class should be high, every other low
  • The goal either way is to
    • maximise the similarity of positive pairs
    • minimise the similarity of negative pairs
  • Without labels, make the vocabulary from the data — the columns become embeddings of other images, and the matched pair is the only positive

Contrastive learning

  • There is a training signal — it is just generated from the data rather than from an annotator
  • Many implementations, differing mainly in how examples are sampled and how the loss is back-propagated
  • What they share: a batch of pairs, a similarity matrix, and a diagonal that is supposed to win
Part two

CLIP

The same idea, with text on the other axis.

CLIP

  • Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining
  • Self-supervised learning on a large dataset of images paired with text captions
  • Reduces the requirement for manual labelling
  • A far wider variety of concepts than a fixed list of 1000 object classes — the vocabulary is whatever people wrote

CLIP — contrastive pre-training

The same matrix as before — but the columns are captions, not other images.
Radford et al. (2021)

How it is trained

  • Trained on WIT: a proprietary dataset of 400 million image–text pairs
  • For a batch of N image–text pairs there are
    • N² possible pairings
    • N true matches — the diagonal
    • N² − N false matches — everything else
  • Measure cosine similarity between the image embedding and the text embedding
  • Contrastive learning with a multi-class N-pair loss
__EQ_COSINEDARK__ __EQ_COSINELIGHT__
Radford et al. (2021)

Using CLIP as a classifier

Turn each class name into a caption, then pick the caption the image is nearest to.
Radford et al. (2021)

Zero-shot classification

CLIP with no training on the task, against a ResNet-50 trained on it.
Radford et al. (2021)

Robustness of CLIP

The gap widens on shifted distributions — sketches, adversarial crops, rendered objects.
Radford et al. (2021)

Why CLIP gets used

  • A particularly useful model for generating embeddings
    • Trained on a very large image dataset (400M pairs)
    • Image and text share one embedding space, so it can generate text pseudo-labels
  • Common uses
    • Auto-labelling large image datasets — LAION was built this way
    • Supplying image embeddings to LLMs
Part three

Visualisation and
interpretation

How do we know what a vision model is thinking?

How do we know what a model is thinking?

  • What does the feature space look like?
  • Where in the image is the model looking?
  • What does one part of it — a neuron, a head — encode?

Visualising embeddings

  • How are images organised in the feature space?
  • It is a high-dimensional space — we cannot simply plot it
  • Two ways round it
    • Reduce the dimensionality, and plot that
    • Or look locally: which images are near neighbours in this space?

Nearest neighbours

Neighbours in feature space share the object, not the pixels — different poses, colours and backgrounds.
Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton (2012)

Dimensionality reduction

  • PCA — principal component analysis
    • Show the dimensions with the most variance
    • Simple, but often hard to interpret: only a few dimensions can be shown at once
  • t-SNE — t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding
    • Flatten high-dimensional data to 2D or 3D so that near neighbours stay nearby
    • Distances between far-apart clusters mean little — read it locally

Feature space visualisation

t-SNE of CNN features: the model has sorted the dataset without being told to.
Karpathy · cs.stanford.edu/people/karpathy/cnnembed/

"Where" is the model looking?

  • Which parts of an image matter most for the class label?
  • Shows what features the model actually uses
  • And it debugs: a model using the background to name the object, or label confusion when there are several objects present

Occlusion

Method and images: Zeiler & Fergus (2014) · scores illustrative

Every position, mapped

African elephant
Go-kart
Dark is where the score fell furthest. The model is looking at the object, not the background it sits in.
Zeiler & Fergus (2014)

CAM — Class Activation Mapping

  • Put a global average pooling layer before the classification layer — GAP averages each channel over x and y
  • The classification weights then say how much each channel matters, and the channel maps say where it fired
Zhou et al. (2016)

CAM — examples

The map is class-specific: ask for a different class and it points somewhere else.
Zhou et al. (2016)

Grad-CAM

Uses the gradient of the class score instead of GAP weights — so it needs no architectural change.
Selvaraju et al. (2016)

"What" does the model encode?

  • What does a particular layer, neuron or attention head encode?
  • Visualise its activation maps or filters, or collect the images that produce a high response
  • For a model like CLIP, you can go further and ask for the text that produces a high response

Decomposing by head and location

Break the output into the contribution of each attention head (what) and each patch (where).
Gandelsman, Efros & Steinhardt (2024)

What each head is for

Each head is summarised by the text nearest its contribution — colour, counting, location, texture.
Gandelsman, Efros & Steinhardt (2024)

What each head is for

These are properties nobody trained for — they fell out of the contrastive objective.
Gandelsman, Efros & Steinhardt (2024)

Nearest neighbours, per head

The same query image has different neighbours depending on which head you ask.
Gandelsman, Efros & Steinhardt (2024)

Three ways of inspecting networks

  • Look at the feature space — near neighbours, PCA, t-SNE
  • Ask where in the image the model is looking — occlusion, CAM, Grad-CAM
  • Ask what a component of the model responds to — a neuron, an attention head
  • All three read the model from the outside; none of them requires retraining it
Part four

Invariance and
generalisation

What survives when the image changes.

Invariance / tolerance

  • Are the features learned by CNNs invariant to lighting, translation, rotation in the image plane, scale, 3D pose?
Image: Han, Zhong, Cao & Zhang (2017)

Classifying from small patches alone

BagNet classifies ImageNet well using small patches alone — no global shape at all.
Brendel & Bethge (2019)

Shape or texture?

Put the texture of one object on the shape of another, and the CNN follows the texture.
Geirhos et al. (2019)

Scrambling the shape barely hurts

VGG-16 on texturised images: 90% → 79%. The global arrangement was carrying almost nothing.
Image: Brendel & Bethge (2019)

Sensitivity to noise

Train on ordinary images and some of these stay easy for people and become impossible for the model.
Image: Geirhos et al. (2018)

Training on one distortion does not transfer

A model retrained on one manipulation handles that one — and is no better on the others.
Image: Geirhos et al. (2018)

Where the training data comes from

Open Images by country of origin. The training distribution is not the world.
Shankar et al. (2017)

And who it comes from

The same object, photographed in a poorer household, is recognised less accurately.
DeVries, Misra, Wang & van der Maaten (2019)

Invariance / tolerance

  • CNNs are tolerant to the variation that was included in their training data
  • They may not generalise to variation outside that distribution — and it is hard to know in advance which is which
  • State-of-the-art training sets carry geographic and wealth biases, and those biases show up as accuracy differences
