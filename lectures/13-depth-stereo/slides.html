Part one

Where depth comes from

A flat image of a three-dimensional world. What survived the projection?

One image, more than one possible room

Projection threw away a dimension, so infinitely many scenes produce this image. The visual system picks one — here, the wrong one.
Ames room

Depth cues in a single view

  • Perspective — parallel lines converge, and equal spacings compress with distance
  • Texture gradient — a uniform texture shrinks and flattens as the surface recedes
  • Occlusion, familiar size, height in the frame — what hides what, and how big things ought to be

Depth cues from more than one view

click, or press →, to play
  • Binocular disparity — two eyes, a fixed baseline apart, see the same point in different places
  • Motion parallax — one eye, moved, is two viewpoints separated in time

Single-view cues are heuristics, two views measure

  • Every single-view cue is a heuristic — that lines really were parallel, that texture really was uniform, that a person really is person-sized
  • Heuristics are usually right, which is why they work, and can be wrong, which is why the Ames room works
  • Two views separated by a known baseline give a measurement instead: the geometry fixes the depth, and no assumption about the scene is needed
The rest of this lecture is that measurement, and its cost: the same point has to be found twice.
Part two

Disparity

Two cameras, side by side, looking the same way.

Parallel planes, equal height, equal focal length

Depth from disparity

  • A world point at depth z images at x in one camera and x′ in the other. Similar triangles relate the shift to the depth
  • The shift x − x′ is the disparity, and it is the only thing in the equation we have to measure
__EQ_DISPARITY__
Depth is inversely proportional to disparity. Near things shift a lot; far things barely move; a point at infinity does not move at all.

Depth error grows with the square of the distance

__EQ_DEPTHEQ__
__EQ_DERIV__ How much does an error measuring d affect the estimate of z?
__EQ_SUBSTD__
  • A disparity error of one pixel becomes a depth error of z²/fb — at twice the distance, four times the error
  • A wider baseline buys precision in direct proportion. So does a longer focal length
  • But a wider baseline also means the two views look less alike, and the match gets harder. That tension never goes away

Alignment makes matching one-dimensional

  • Everything now rests on measuring x − x′, which means finding the same world point in both images. That is the correspondence problem from last lecture
  • But alignment has already done most of the work: a point on row v of the left image can only appear on row v of the right image
  • The search collapses from a whole image to a single line, and from thousands of candidates to a few hundred
  • Which is why we can afford to do it at every pixel, rather than at a few hundred keypoints
Why the match lies on that row and not some other line is next lecture's subject.
Part three

Finding correspondences

One line to search, and four ways for the search to go wrong.

Scan the row and take the best match

  • For each pixel x in the left image, take the window around it
  • Slide that window along the same row of the right image and score every position
  • Take the best-scoring x′, and the disparity gives the depth

SSD, and why normalised correlation instead

__EQ_SSD__
  • Sum of squared differences: cheap, and the obvious first choice
__EQ_NCC__
  • Normalised correlation subtracts the mean and divides by the norm, so it is unchanged by a gain and offset on either window
  • Two cameras rarely agree on exposure, white balance or black level. SSD reads that disagreement as a mismatch; NCC does not

Matching along a scanline, live

Window size trades detail against noise

A small window keeps edges and picks up noise. A large window is smooth and blurs the boundaries it was meant to measure.
Images: S. Seitz

Where window matching fails

  • No texture — a blank wall matches everywhere equally well
  • Repeated texture — a brick wall matches equally well in several places
  • Occlusion — the point is visible in one view and hidden in the other, so the right answer is not on the row at all
  • Specular surfaces — the highlight lives at a different place on the object in each view, so the two images disagree about what is even there
Images: Muryy, Fleming & Welchman (2014)

Window matching alone is noisy everywhere

The estimated depth recovers the large-scale structure and is wrong in detail almost everywhere. Each pixel was decided on its own, and nothing made neighbouring answers agree.
Images: S. Seitz

Uniqueness, ordering and smoothness

  • Uniqueness — a point in one view has at most one match in the other
  • Ordering — points along a row keep their left-to-right order in both views. Usually. A thin object in front of a distant background breaks it, and so does anything transparent
  • Smoothness — disparity changes slowly, except at the object boundaries where it must not
None of these is about one pixel. They are all statements about the answer as a whole, which means the answer has to be found as a whole.

Match quality plus smoothness, minimised globally

__EQ_ENERGY__
  • The first term asks every pixel to match well. The second asks neighbours to agree
  • λ is the exchange rate between them: too small and the map stays noisy, too large and the object boundaries are smoothed away with the noise
  • ρ grows slowly, so one genuine depth discontinuity is expensive but not forbidden
Every pixel's answer now depends on its neighbours', so there is no longer a per-pixel decision to make. The whole image has to be solved at once.

The energy is the cost of a cut

What solving it globally buys

The same matching costs as before. What changed is that the answers were required to agree with each other.

Part four

Learning depth from one image

If heuristics are what a single view has, learn them.

Depth as a per-pixel prediction

  • Predict a distance for every pixel; train on images with measured depth maps, from a depth camera or LiDAR
  • Depths span orders of magnitude, so a loss on log depth behaves far better than one on depth
  • Scenes differ hugely in mean depth — a wardrobe against a stadium. A scale-invariant loss stops the model just learning the average
Images: NYU Depth Dataset V2

A coarse network, then a refinement network

One network fixes the overall layout of the scene; a second adds the detail it cannot see. The global cue and the local cue are learned separately.
Eigen, Puhrsch & Fergus (2014)

Supervision from stereo pairs, not depth maps

  • Predict the disparity that would turn the left image into the right one, and score the reconstruction. No depth maps needed — only a stereo rig
  • Loss is appearance + disparity smoothness + left-right consistency, which catches occlusions the appearance term cannot
Godard, Mac Aodha & Brostow (2017)

Blurred edges, narrow generalisation, borrowed scale

  • Edges come out blurred: the network is interpolating a smooth field, and a depth discontinuity is exactly what that cannot represent
  • It generalises only as far as its training distribution. A model trained indoors has learned what indoor rooms are like, not what depth is
  • It reports metres, but the metres are not in the image — scene and focal length scale together, giving an identical picture. The scale comes from an assumed focal length: get that wrong and every depth is out by the same factor
  • And it is still doing the thing the Ames room defeats: applying heuristics, now learned from data rather than built in

Summary

  • A single view carries plenty of depth cues, and every one of them is an assumption that can be broken
  • Two aligned views give a measurement: z = fb/d, with precision falling as z²
  • The work is all in the matching, which is ambiguous pixel by pixel and only becomes well posed when solved as a whole image
  • Learning can recover depth from one image, but inherits the limits of what it was trained on — and its scale is assumed, not measured
Two debts to next lecture: why the match lies on a row, and where the rectifying homography comes from.
